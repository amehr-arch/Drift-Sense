# Justification of augmentation and noise choices

Every structural parameter, noise term and augmentation in the generator is listed
below with the reasoning behind its *form* and the sources that support it.

Where a published source exists it is cited in full — author list, title, venue,
volume, pages and year, with a DOI or ISBN where one exists and a full-text link
where the authors host an open copy. A few sections describe choices taken directly
from the organisers' released metadata or from standard practice with no single
canonical reference; those are marked as such and give search terms rather than a
citation.

---

## 1. SEM image formation

### 1.1 Secondary-electron yield and material contrast
**Implemented in:** `imaging.secondary_electron_signal`, `imaging.DEFAULT_YIELDS`

Image brightness is set by the number of secondary electrons escaping the surface,
which varies with material. Metal features emit more strongly than dielectric or
bare silicon, so contacts and metal lines appear brighter than the surrounding
field. The generator assigns a relative yield per material and forms the pixel
value as the area-weighted mean over the materials visible in that pixel.

The relative *ordering* of the yields matters far more than the exact values,
which is why they are exposed as parameters rather than hard-coded.

- Reimer, L., *Scanning Electron Microscopy: Physics of Image Formation and Microanalysis*, 2nd edn, Springer Series in Optical Sciences vol. 45, Springer, Berlin/Heidelberg, 1998, xiv + 529 pp. ISBN 978-3-540-63976-3. DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-38967-5 — the standard treatment of SE emission and contrast mechanisms; ch. 4 for emission, ch. 6 for the contrast that follows from it.
- Goldstein, J. I., Newbury, D. E., Michael, J. R., Ritchie, N. W. M., Scott, J. H. J., Joy, D. C., *Scanning Electron Microscopy and X-Ray Microanalysis*, 4th edn, Springer, New York, 2018, xxiii + 550 pp. ISBN 978-1-4939-6674-5. DOI 10.1007/978-1-4939-6676-9 — ch. 3 for secondary electrons, ch. 7 for image interpretation.

### 1.2 Edge brightening
**Implemented in:** `imaging.edge_density`, and the fringe scaling in `imaging.form_image`

SE yield rises on a tilted surface roughly as the secant law, δ ∝ 1/cos θ: tilting
lengthens the primary-beam path *within* the shallow escape layer, so a larger
fraction of the interaction volume lies within one escape depth of a surface. At a
feature edge there is a second effect — the beam lands near the edge and secondaries
escape through the sidewall as well as through the top face. Top-down images
therefore show a bright fringe along every feature edge. This is the single most
recognisable characteristic of an SEM micrograph and the problem statement calls
it out explicitly as mandatory.

Two modelling decisions are worth stating:

- The driver is the *length of material edge* within a pixel, estimated as the
  gradient magnitude of the coverage fields. This is a length-per-area quantity
  and so is resolution dependent by construction.
- The brightness contribution is that length times a fixed physical fringe width,
  divided by pixel area. Without this scaling the term brightens a 10 nm/px
  capture roughly ten times too much, since a coarse pixel contains more edge.

  The fringe width parameter is currently set from the SE escape depth, which is
  3.8–5.4 nm in silicon and 1–5 nm in metals — though 10–20 nm in insulators, and a
  DRAM stack contains a good deal of oxide and nitride. It should be read as a lower
  bound rather than a physical prediction: the fringe width actually observed in a
  CD-SEM is dominated by the probe diameter and by the SE2 component generated by
  exiting backscattered electrons, whose lateral range is tens to hundreds of
  nanometres at typical landing energies. A wider fringe would be the more faithful
  choice; the narrow setting is a deliberately conservative one.

- Reimer (1998), as above, §4.2.1 (dependence of SE yield on beam and specimen parameters, i.e. the tilt dependence) and §§6.1.2, 6.2.1 (topographic contrast in the SE and BSE modes).
- Search terms for further support: `SEM edge effect secondary electron emission sidewall`, `secondary electron escape depth silicon`.

### 1.3 Beam point spread and astigmatism
**Implemented in:** `imaging.gaussian_blur`, `CaptureParams.spot_size_nm`, `astigmatism_ratio`

The probe has finite size, so the image is the specimen convolved with the beam
profile, modelled as a Gaussian. Unequal widths on the two axes represent residual
astigmatism, the dominant aberration in a slightly mis-tuned column and a
parameter the organisers' own sample metadata exposes.

The wide-field capture is given a larger spot than the high-magnification one,
which is the normal operating trade-off: lower magnification is usually run with
more probe current and a correspondingly larger spot.

- Goldstein et al. (2018), as above, ch. 5 (SEM instrumentation: electron guns, lens system, probe size and current, aberrations), with the imaging consequences in ch. 6 and ch. 10.
- Search terms: `SEM point spread function probe size resolution`, `astigmatism correction scanning electron microscope`.

### 1.4 Shot noise and dose
**Implemented in:** `imaging.apply_dose_noise`, `CaptureParams.dose`

The dominant noise source. The number of secondary electrons detected per pixel is
a Poisson variable whose mean is proportional to dose, so signal-to-noise scales as
the square root of dose. This is why the wide-field image is noisier: it collects
about a tenth of the electrons per pixel, giving roughly three times the relative
noise, not ten times.

Modelling this as Poisson rather than as additive Gaussian is what makes the noise
*signal-dependent*, which is the behaviour real low-dose SEM images show.

The 10:1 dose ratio and the 2.0/5.0 detector-noise ratio are taken directly from
the metadata block released with the organisers' sample image pair.

- Reimer (1998), as above, §4.2.4 (statistics of BSE and SE emission) and §6.3 (resolution and contrast in the SE and BSE modes, i.e. the dose–resolution relation).
- Goldstein et al. (2018), as above, ch. 8, "The Visibility of Features in SEM Images", for the threshold-current/contrast argument.
- Search terms: `Poisson shot noise SEM low dose signal to noise`, `CD-SEM measurement uncertainty frame averaging`.

### 1.5 Detector and readout noise
**Implemented in:** `CaptureParams.detector_noise_sigma`

Additive, signal-independent noise from the amplifier chain, applied *after* shot
noise because it originates downstream in the detection path. Modelled as Gaussian
in grey levels.

- Reimer (1998), as above, §5.5, "Detector Noise" (5.5.1 scintillator–photomultiplier, 5.5.2 semiconductor detectors).

### 1.6 Specimen charging
**Implemented in:** `imaging.apply_charging`

Charge accumulating on a poorly grounded feature deflects the beam and inflates
the collected signal for the remainder of that scan line, decaying as the charge
bleeds away. The artefact is therefore *directional*: it follows the raster rather
than the geometry, which is why it is applied as horizontal streaks with an
exponential decay rather than as an isotropic blob.

The organisers' metadata exposes `charging_streak_prob` and
`charging_streak_intensity`, and their FAQ names charging explicitly.

- No single citation; see Goldstein et al. (2018), ch. 9, "Image Defects", and ch. 12 on variable-pressure SEM, where charging is the motivating problem.
- Search terms: `SEM specimen charging artifacts insulating samples`, `charging induced image distortion scanning electron microscopy`.

### 1.7 Vignetting and gamma
**Implemented in:** `imaging.apply_vignette`, `CaptureParams.gamma`

Collection efficiency falls off away from the optical axis, applied before
detection because it scales the collected signal. Gamma is a display transfer
applied after noise, since it does not affect the physics of detection. Both
appear in the organisers' metadata schema. No external citation; these follow the
organisers' schema and standard display practice.

## 2. Device structure

### 2.1 DRAM cell architecture
**Implemented in:** `layouts.dram.CELL_ARCHITECTURES`, `DramParams`

Word lines run horizontally and gate the access transistors; bit lines run
vertically; a storage-node contact sits at each crossing. Cell dimensions are
expressed in the lithographic feature size `F`, and cells are named for their area
in units of `F²`:

| Architecture | Cell | Bit-line pitch | Word-line pitch |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6F² | 2F × 3F | 2F | 3F |
| 8F² | 2F × 4F | 2F | 4F |

**Convention, stated explicitly because the literature carries two.** The table
above uses the *circuit-architecture* convention, in which the quoted word-line
pitch is the pitch of *addressing* word lines — the spacing between the lines that
actually gate a cell. This is the convention used in standard DRAM design teaching
material (Stanford CS448A gives verbatim "Cell Size 8F2, WL pitch: 4F, BL pitch: 2F"
for folded bit lines and "Cell Size 6F2, WL pitch: 3F, BL pitch: 2F" for open bit
lines).

The *layout/process* convention differs, and the difference is visible in a
micrograph. There F is the half-pitch of the drawn lines, so every minimum-pitch
line sits at a 2F pitch — including word lines. In a folded 8F² array the 4F is the
cell length along the bit line, spanning **two** word-line pitches, because a passing
word line runs as field polysilicon across the cell without gating it. That passing
line is a real drawn feature and a top-down SEM sees it. If the generator renders
word lines at a 4F pitch it omits half the lines physically present on the wafer.

Two further caveats: the 2F bit-line pitch is not universal — Micron's 6F² designs
put the relaxed pitch on the digit line instead (3F bit-line pitch, 2F word-line
pitch), the transpose of the table above — and the 6F²/8F² area figures are correct
under either convention.

Both architectures are implemented. The generator samples `F` over 24–48 nm, a
deliberately wide range so that no single pitch can be learned.

- IEEE International Roadmap for Devices and Systems (IRDS™), *More Moore*, 2023 Update, IEEE, 2023. <https://irds.ieee.org/images/files/pdf/2023/2023IRDS_MM.pdf> — current, citable ground rules and pitches. (The most recent *More Moore* chapter published is the 2024 Edition, <https://irds.ieee.org/images/files/pdf/2024/2024IRDS_MM.pdf>.)
- Stanford CS448A, *DRAM Design Overview*, lecture notes, 2001. <https://graphics.stanford.edu/courses/cs448a-01-fall/lectures/dram/dram.2up.pdf> — source of the 6F²/8F² pitch convention used above.
- Search terms: `DRAM 6F2 8F2 cell architecture word line bit line`, `DRAM array layout storage node contact`.

### 2.2 Mat structure
**Implemented in:** `DramParams.mat_size_nm`, `strip_width_nm`

Arrays are not continuous across a die; they are broken into mats (sub-arrays)
separated by periphery carrying sense amplifiers and sub-word-line drivers.
Defaults of 2600 nm and 320 nm come from the organisers' released sample metadata,
and at a 10 µm field of view produce about 3.4 mats per axis
(10000 / (2600 + 320) = 3.42), which matches the mat count visible in their sample
search image.

Mat boundaries matter far beyond realism: they are the only features that break
translational periodicity, and therefore the only unambiguous anchors a locator
can use. See §4.

### 2.3 Per-mat variation
**Implemented in:** `DramParams.mat_pitch_variation`, `layouts.dram.mat_jitter`

Each mat column carries its own bit-line pitch and each mat row its own word-line
pitch. This is visible in the organisers' own sample search image, whose mats
differ noticeably in pitch and contrast rather than being carbon copies.

It is also a correctness requirement rather than a cosmetic one. With identical
mats, a reference window from one mat is pixel-identical to the same window from
another, and the pair is unsolvable by construction. Measured on an early dataset,
the ground truth was not the global correlation maximum on 8 of 12 pairs.

### 2.4 Etch bias
**Implemented in:** `DramParams.linewidth_bias_nm`

Printed features differ systematically from drawn dimensions through resist and
etch processing. Modelled as a uniform growth or shrink applied to every drawn
feature, clamped so a line can never swallow its pitch. Present in the organisers'
metadata as `linewidth_bias_nm`.

- Mack, C. A., *Fundamental Principles of Optical Lithography: The Science of Microfabrication*, Wiley, Chichester, November 2007, 544 pp. ISBN 978-0-470-01893-4. DOI 10.1002/9780470723876 — CD control, bias, and the printed-versus-drawn distinction.

### 2.5 Line-edge roughness
**Implemented in:** `roughness.RoughnessParams`, `roughness.apply_roughness`

Printed edges are not straight. Photon shot noise during exposure, the finite
size of resist polymer aggregates and stochastic acid diffusion leave an edge
that wanders about its nominal position by a nanometre or two, correlated along
the line over tens of nanometres. Defaults are a 1.2 nm standard deviation
(1σ — equivalently 3.6 nm 3σ, which is how the lithography literature quotes it)
with a 40 nm correlation length along the line and 12 nm across lines.

Two honest notes on how those defaults sit against the literature:

- The 3.6 nm 3σ amplitude is inside the published range for resist-level roughness
  (Mack & Bristol measure 2.58 nm 3σ LER on a 36 nm-pitch EUV grating; production
  EUV reporting runs 4–5 nm 3σ). It is on the high side for *post-etch* features,
  which are usually smoother than the resist they were printed from.
- The 40 nm correlation length is above the published range. Mack gives "10 to 30 nm
  correlation lengths typical of high-resolution photoresist processes", and the
  measured EUV value is ≈ 8 nm; ≈ 41 nm appears in the literature only *after*
  roughness post-processing. The default therefore produces longer-wavelength,
  visibly smoother edge wander than a real line. 10–30 nm would be the better-
  supported default.

The model is a displacement field applied to the rendered coverage, evaluated
analytically at world coordinates so that the same physical point is displaced
identically at 1 nm/px and at 10 nm/px. It is anisotropic by construction:
correlated along a line and independent between neighbouring lines, which are
separate stochastic events. An isotropic field would make adjacent lines wander
together, which is stage drift rather than roughness.

Crucially it is applied to the *specimen*, not to each capture. Both images see
the same rough edges, because they are looking at the same silicon.

**Sources**

- Mack (2007), as in §2.4 — stochastic effects in resist and the origins of edge roughness.
- Mack, C. A., Bristol, R. L., "Line-edge roughness performance targets for EUV lithography", *Proc. SPIE* 10143, *Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) Lithography VIII*, 2017. <https://lithoguru.com/scientist/litho_papers/2017_LER_Performance_Targets_for_EUV.pdf> — measured LER amplitude and correlation length.
- Reimer (1998), as above — what an SEM actually resolves of such an edge.

## 3. Stage and scan error

### 3.1 Inter-visit stage error
**Implemented in:** `imaging.apply_geometric_error`

The reason the problem exists. Motion stages accumulate positioning error between
visits from thermal drift, vibration and mechanical slack, so a tool returning to a
site lands slightly off and at a slightly different angle. The webinar puts the
rotation at roughly 1–3°.

Modelled as rotation, scale error and shear applied to the reference capture. The
attribution is deliberate: the answer is expressed in search-image coordinates, so
the search image defines the frame and any *relative* misalignment belongs to the
other image. This keeps the ground truth exactly computable while still presenting
the locator with the full relative distortion.

- No external citation; the magnitude is taken from the organisers' webinar.
- Search terms: `wafer stage positioning error thermal drift precision motion stage`, `die to die registration wafer inspection navigation`.

### 3.2 Scan drift jitter
**Implemented in:** `CaptureParams.drift_jitter_px`

The stage creeps between scan lines, so each raster line is displaced
horizontally by an independent small amount. Applied per row rather than as a
smooth field, which is what makes it distinguishable from a global shift.
`drift_jitter_px` appears in the organisers' metadata.

## 4. Deliberate ambiguity

**Implemented in:** `sampling.PlacementSampler`, `generate.GeneratedPair.anchor`

Not an augmentation but a property of the data worth documenting. Inside a mat the
array is perfectly periodic, so a reference window lying wholly within one is
ambiguous up to translation by one cell pitch. No algorithm can resolve it from
the two images alone.

This is a true property of the problem, not a generator defect. It is why the
problem statement specifies a nearest-to-centre tiebreak, and why the organisers
say their test set contains a region where correct localisation is genuinely
difficult. Rather than engineering it away, every pair is labelled with whether its
reference window contains a structural anchor on each axis, and evaluation reports
the classes separately.

## 5b. Optical image formation (bonus scope)
**Implemented in:** `optical.OpticalParams`, `optical.form_rgb_image`

Diffraction sets the resolution of an optical microscope at the Rayleigh limit,
`0.61 * lambda / NA`. At 550 nm and NA 0.9 that is about 370 nm. Against a
bit-line pitch of 2F over the sampled F range of 24–48 nm — so 48 to 96 nm — that is
roughly four to eight times the pitch, about five times at mid-range. The cell array
is therefore not resolved at all and only mat-scale structure survives. Blur is
applied per channel because the limit scales with wavelength; blue resolves finer
than red.

Colour is modelled as a flat per-material reflectance rather than from a thin-film
stack. Real optical colour on a wafer comes mostly from interference in the
dielectric stack, which needs a film model this project does not have. The
consequence -- that the three channels are close to scaled copies -- is stated in
the module rather than hidden.

**Sources**

- Mack (2007), as in §2.4 -- imaging, numerical aperture and the diffraction limit.
- Goldstein et al. (2018), as above -- for the contrast with electron optics.

## 6. What is deliberately not modelled yet

Recorded so the gaps are visible rather than accidental.

- **A measured LER power spectral density.** The roughness model (section 2.5) uses a band-limited sum of sinusoids with a specified correlation length rather than a fitted PSD from a particular process. Matching a published PSD curve would be the next refinement — and would also settle the correlation-length question raised in §2.5.
- **The SE2 contribution to edge fringe width.** The fringe kernel is set from the SE escape depth alone (§1.2), which is a lower bound; the backscattered-electron-driven SE2 component that widens real fringes is not modelled.
- **The passing word line.** Under the layout convention noted in §2.1, a folded array carries a field-polysilicon word line across each cell that the current 4F-pitch rendering omits.
- **Corner rounding.** Printed corners are rounded by the optics and the resist. Contacts are currently square.
- **Periphery circuitry.** The strips between mats render as flat substrate; real periphery carries sense amplifiers and drivers, which would add useful non-periodic structure.
- **Gate cut masks, dummy gates and metal-1 routing** are not modelled in the FinFET layout. All would add non-periodic detail and so make localisation *easier*; leaving them out keeps it the harder case.
